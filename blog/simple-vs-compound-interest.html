Simple vs. Compound Interest: What Actually Changes Your Total

Published 21 July 2026 · 5 min read

"Simple" and "compound" interest sound like a minor technical distinction, but over long enough time periods they produce dramatically different totals from the exact same starting numbers. Knowing which one applies to a loan, savings account, or investment you're looking at is one of the most useful pieces of financial literacy there is.

Simple interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, every period, for the life of the loan or deposit:

Interest = Principal × Rate × Time

The interest earned (or owed) in year two is identical to year one, because it's always calculated against the same starting amount. This is the formula behind our Simple Interest Calculator.

Compound interest

Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus whatever interest has already accumulated. Each period's interest gets added to the balance before the next period's interest is calculated:

Total = Principal × (1 + Rate)Time

This is why compound interest is often described as "interest on interest" — the base it's calculated against keeps growing.

A worked example

Say you invest $10,000 at a 6% annual rate for 20 years.

Method Total after 20 years
Simple interest$22,000
Compound interest (annual)$32,071

Same principal, same rate, same time period — but compounding produces over $10,000 more, purely because each year's interest starts earning its own interest. The gap grows faster the longer the time horizon, which is why long-term retirement accounts benefit so heavily from starting early.

Why this matters for borrowing, not just saving

The same math works against you on debt. A credit card that compounds interest monthly on an unpaid balance accumulates faster than the "simple" version of the same annual rate would suggest, which is part of why credit card balances can grow so quickly if only minimum payments are made.

The takeaway

Before comparing two savings or loan offers, check whether the quoted rate is simple or compound — and if compound, how often it's applied (monthly compounding produces a higher effective total than annual compounding at the same nominal rate). That single detail matters more than most of the fine print around it.
