How to Calculate Your BMI Accurately (And What It Doesn't Tell You)

By the NKRHUB Editorial Team · Published 21 July 2026 · 5 min read

Body Mass Index shows up on medical forms, fitness apps, and insurance questionnaires, but most people have never actually seen the formula behind it — they just type numbers into a calculator and take the label it spits out at face value. Understanding the formula makes the result far more useful, because you'll know exactly what it's measuring and, just as importantly, what it isn't.

The formula

BMI is weight divided by height squared:

BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²

If you're working in pounds and inches, the imperial version multiplies by a conversion constant:

BMI = 703 × weight (lb) ÷ height (in)²

Both formulas produce the same result — they're just unit conversions of each other. Our BMI Calculator handles the conversion automatically, so you don't have to remember the constant.

Where people get inaccurate results

The formula itself is simple, but the inputs are where accuracy usually breaks down:

  • Height measured with shoes on. This can add a full centimeter or more, which shifts the result more than people expect at the margins between categories.
  • Weight measured at different times of day. Body weight can fluctuate by a kilogram or two over 24 hours due to hydration and food intake. Measuring at a consistent time (e.g., morning, before eating) gives more comparable results over weeks.
  • Mixing unit systems. Entering height in centimeters but weight in pounds, or similar mismatches, silently produces a meaningless number.

The standard categories

BMI Range Category
Below 18.5Underweight
18.5 – 24.9Normal weight
25.0 – 29.9Overweight
30.0 and aboveObese

These are the World Health Organization's general adult categories. They don't adjust for age, sex, or muscle mass.

What BMI doesn't measure

BMI was designed in the 1830s as a population-level statistical tool, not an individual diagnostic one. It can't distinguish muscle from fat — a heavily muscled athlete and a sedentary person of the same height and weight will get the identical BMI, despite very different body compositions. It also doesn't account for where fat is distributed on the body, which matters for some health risk assessments. Doctors typically use BMI as a quick first-pass screening number, then follow up with other measurements (waist circumference, body fat percentage, bloodwork) if something looks worth investigating further.

The takeaway

Use BMI as a rough, consistent way to track trends over time for yourself — not as a verdict. Measure under the same conditions each time, and treat a single number less seriously than a trend across several weeks or months.

Sources and review notes
